Planck's constant from the photoelectric effect

A short companion to the simulation: the physics behind the readings, the parts of the instrument, and the usual ways a measurement goes wrong.

The effect and the photon

Light that falls on a metal can knock electrons out of it. Classical waves would predict that a brighter beam of any colour should do the job. What is seen is different: below a certain frequency nothing is emitted, however bright the light, and above it the electrons leave with an energy that depends on the colour, not on the brightness.

Einstein explained this by treating light as a stream of quanta. Each photon carries an energy set by its frequency:

E = h·ν

where h is Planck's constant. One photon gives all of its energy to one electron, or none.

Energy balance

To leave the metal an electron must pay a fixed price, the work function W. What is left over becomes motion:

E_kin = h·ν − W

The fastest electrons are the ones that lose nothing on the way out. Brighter light only means more electrons, not faster ones.

The limit voltage U0

In this experiment the electrons that reach the ring anode charge a small capacitor (100 pF). The growing voltage pushes back on the next electrons, as a hill that gets steeper. The charging slows, and it stops when even the fastest electrons can no longer climb. That voltage is the limit voltage U0, and the capacitor sits there because the current into it has fallen to zero. In the ideal case the current falls to zero exactly at U0. In a real cell some charge always leaks away, so the capacitor stops where the photocurrent equals the leakage current, a little below U0: the reading flattens into a plateau.

At U0 the electron's kinetic energy has been spent exactly on the climb. In its simplest, naive form:

e·U₀ = h·ν − W

This is the naive form. As the section on the intercept shows, the W that really appears here turns out to be the anode's, not the cathode's.

Why the slope gives h

Measure U0 for several spectral lines and plot it against frequency. The relation above is a straight line:

ΔU₀ / Δν = h / e

so h is the slope multiplied by the elementary charge e. Classroom results are often 5–10 % low, as in the leaflet's own result (6.1·10⁻³⁴ J·s), against the accepted value of 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

Uncertainty and the fit

Every reading has an uncertainty σ. For the analog needle the simulation assumes you can read about a fifth of the smallest scale division: 0.02 V on the 3 V range. In Explore the digital readout is good to about 1 mV. In Lab you may type your own ± value next to the reading.

Measure the same line several times (discharging each time) and the readings scatter a little. For each line the table gives the mean, the spread s and the standard error s/√n. The error bar of the mean, used in the fit, is the larger of s and σ, divided by √n: averaging shrinks the random part, but with only a few readings s itself is uncertain, so it is never allowed to fall below what the needle can resolve.

The fit weighs each line by 1/σ², so a well-measured line pulls harder than a doubtful one, and gives h ± σh together with χ² per degree of freedom (near 1 when the error bars match the scatter; shown from three lines on). Compare your h with 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s in units of σ: within 1σ or 2σ the result agrees; more than 2σ away points to a systematic error, such as leakage or stray light.

The multimeter's accuracy class is a separate, systematic uncertainty. A calibration (gain) error scales all readings by the same factor, so it tilts the whole line by that fraction (about 2.5 % in h for the assumed class) instead of scattering the points; it is therefore not drawn in the error bars and averaging cannot remove it. A constant offset error would shift the line and change the intercept, not the slope.

The intercept is not the cathode's work function

It is tempting to read the intercept of the line as the work function of the potassium cathode. It is not. Cathode and anode are connected through the circuit, so their Fermi levels coincide at U = 0 (the charged capacitor then shifts the anode levels by e·U0). The anode surface is a different material with its own work function WA, and the electron has to get over the anode's barrier, plus the offset e·U0 from the capacitor:

h·ν = W_A + e·U₀

The intercept therefore measures WA, a value that depends on crystal face and adsorbed gas and is hard to know. The cathode's own work function never appears in the slope or the intercept. What the cathode does set is the emission threshold: for this photocell WK/h is about 459 THz, below which no electrons come out at all. The straight line crosses U0 = 0 at a different frequency, about WA/h ≈ 370 THz in the simulation, and that extrapolated crossing is not the emission threshold.

Electrically, the cell feeds a 100 pF capacitor. An electrometer amplifier lets the analog multimeter (3 V DC range) read the capacitor voltage without draining it, and a normally-open key switch discharges the capacitor before each reading.

Procedure

  1. Darken the room a little and set the multimeter to 3 V DC.
  2. Turn the swivelling arm until the chosen spectral line falls on the photocell. If the room is too bright, slide the cover (12) over the emission window: it keeps room light out of the cell and does not weaken the spectral line on the cathode.
  3. Hold the key until the meter reads zero, so the capacitor is empty.
  4. Release the key and wait for the voltage to flatten out, about half a minute to a minute. Note U₀.
  5. Repeat for each line: yellow, green, blue and violet. After changing line, discharge the capacitor again before releasing the key.
  6. Plot U₀ against frequency, fit a line, and multiply the slope by e.

Sources of error

  • Stray light. Dust or fingerprints on the optics scatter light of other colours onto the cell. Low-frequency lines, which should give a small U₀, read too high.
  • Leakage. A dirty cell lets charge creep from anode to cathode, so the plateau settles lower than the true U₀. It is worse for weak light and for high U₀ (the photocurrent is small there, so the leakage wins sooner), so the high-frequency points fall more and the slope, and with it h, comes out low.
  • Reading too early. The capacitor is still charging. Wait until the needle stops drifting.
  • Disturbing the setup. The capacitor is sensitive to induction: avoid moving around or touching the equipment while measuring.

Glossary

Photon
A quantum of light with energy h·ν.
Work function (W)
The minimum energy needed to remove an electron from a surface.
Limit voltage (U₀)
The retarding voltage at which the photocurrent drops to zero.
Fermi level
The highest filled electron energy in a metal at rest.
Spectral line
One narrow colour of the mercury lamp, with a single wavelength. The yellow line is really a doublet (577 and 579 nm) that the simulation treats as one line.
Electrometer amplifier
A very high input resistance buffer that lets a meter read a small capacitor without loading it.
